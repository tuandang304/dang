\begin{abstract}
Polyp segmentation networks that reach about 93\% mean Dice on in-domain colonoscopy images degrade when only a few frames are labelled, on tiny or low-contrast lesions, and under changes in acquisition conditions. We present DANG, a segmentation network that pairs a trainable ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder with a frozen DINOv2-Base foundation model and uses the foundation features through three components: a dual-prototype margin head, a prototype-anchored spatial support envelope that suppresses false positives far from the lesion, and an uncertainty-guided strip decoder. We train on 40 or 800 Kvasir-SEG images, test on deduplicated external and stress cohorts over five paired random seeds, and retrain PraNet, Polyp-PVT and EMCAD under the same protocol. With 40 labelled images DANG leads every retrained model on all external evaluations, by at least 7.1 percentage points of mean Dice and 4.8 points of boundary F1, and most of these differences remain significant after Holm correction. A control that adds the same frozen DINOv2 by feature concatenation stays at baseline level, so the frozen features alone do not explain the gain. With 800 labelled images DANG is statistically tied with Polyp-PVT and EMCAD. On the standard 1,450-image training split it matches a faithful Polyp-PVT reproduction on four test sets and exceeds it on ETIS-LaribPolypDB (84.4\% versus 78.9\% mean Dice; three seeds, uncorrected). These gains cost inference speed: DANG runs at 17 frames per second on an NVIDIA T4, two to two and a half times slower than the retrained models.
\end{abstract}

Colonoscopy, deep learning, foundation models, label-efficient learning, medical image segmentation, polyp segmentation, prototype learning, robustness.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{C}{olorectal} cancer is the third most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide~\cite{sung2021globocan}, and removing adenomatous polyps during colonoscopy lowers colorectal-cancer mortality~\cite{zauber2012polypectomy}. Pixel-level polyp segmentation supports this by delineating lesion margins. On the in-domain benchmark Kvasir-SEG~\cite{jha2020kvasir}, however, a plain ConvNeXt-Tiny~\cite{liu2022convnet} with a Feature Pyramid Network~\cite{lin2017fpn} already reaches about $93\%$ mDice, and in our experiments the proposed model and this baseline differ by $0.03$~pp in domain (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). In-domain scores therefore say little about robustness.

Deployment stresses a model along three axes that in-domain benchmarks do not exercise:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scarce labels.} Pixel-accurate contours require expert time, so a new centre may label only tens of frames. Trained on $40$ images, CNNs overfit to source textures and their boundaries degrade on external centres.
  \item \textbf{Tiny and camouflaged lesions.} Flat or diminutive polyps covering under $1.5\%$ of the frame, or with low contrast to the surrounding mucosa, are fragmented or missed by stride-$16/32$ features, especially on ETIS-LaribPolypDB~\cite{silva2014toward} and CVC-ColonDB~\cite{tajbakhsh2015automated}.
  \item \textbf{Acquisition shift.} Procedures switch between white-light and NBI-like spectra and suffer illumination fall-off, sensor noise, specular glare and motion blur.
\end{enumerate}

Vision foundation models such as DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} and SAM~\cite{kirillov2023segment} provide features that transfer across domains, but their $14$- or $16$-pixel patch grids are too coarse for lesion boundaries. Concatenating such features with a CNN is the obvious remedy; we show that it does not help when labels are scarce. \emph{We therefore do not target in-domain state of the art; we ask how frozen foundation features should be used when only a few frames are labelled.}

We present \textbf{DANG} (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) and make three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A decoder that uses foundation features through prototypes.} Multi-depth DINOv2 tokens feed a dual-prototype margin head with test-time self-support; the \textbf{prototype-anchored spatial support envelope (PASSE)} turns this margin into a mask that suppresses false positives far from the lesion and reinforces low-contrast lesion cores; and an uncertainty-guided strip decoder refines boundaries where the coarse prediction is ambiguous.
  \item \textbf{A controlled evaluation protocol.} A deduplicated multi-centre benchmark with a $40$-label training regime and two stress cohorts (Figure~\ref{fig:examples}); PraNet~\cite{fan2020pranet}, Polyp-PVT~\cite{dong2021polyp} and EMCAD~\cite{rahman2024emcad} retrained under the same data, schedule and evaluation; five paired seeds with Holm-corrected tests; and a standard-split experiment that links the protocol to published numbers.
  \item \textbf{Findings, positive and negative.} A same-size concatenation control shows that the frozen features alone do not explain DANG's gain. DANG leads all retrained models with $40$ labels, ties the strongest of them with $800$ labels, and costs $2$--$2.5\times$ their inference time; its Retinex stem shows no measurable gain.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Polyp segmentation.} Architectures progressed from U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} and UNet++~\cite{zhou2018unetplusplus} to reverse-attention networks: PraNet~\cite{fan2020pranet} mines boundary cues with parallel partial decoders and reverse attention, and CaraNet~\cite{lou2022caranet} adds axial context for small objects. Transformer encoders improved multi-centre generalisation in Polyp-PVT~\cite{dong2021polyp} and TransFuse~\cite{zhang2021transfuse}, and EMCAD~\cite{rahman2024emcad} pairs a PVTv2 encoder with an efficient multi-scale convolutional attention decoder. Label-efficient work on polyps has mostly been semi-supervised; FPGM~\cite{xi2025fpgm}, for example, uses a frequency prior on polyp edges to augment unlabelled images and improve generalisation to new centres. DANG instead uses no unlabelled data and draws its prior from a frozen foundation model.

\paragraph{Foundation models for polyps.} SAM applied without prompts segments polyps poorly~\cite{zhou2023samPolyps}, and MedSAM~\cite{ma2024medsam} fine-tunes SAM on a large medical corpus but requires box prompts. SAM-Mamba~\cite{dutta2024sammamba} and SAM-MaGuP~\cite{dutta2025sammagup} adapt SAM with Mamba-based priors for zero-shot polyp generalisation, at about $103$M parameters and $423$~GFLOPs~\cite{agnihotri2026litebound}. A second line distils foundation models into small networks: Polyp-DiFoM~\cite{agnihotri2025difom} distils SAM and DINOv2 into lightweight baselines, LiteBounD~\cite{agnihotri2026litebound} adds boundary-guided frequency-aware distillation, and Lite-$\pi$~\cite{agnihotri2026litepi} aligns foundation-model-specific prototypes with a lightweight network for real-time inference. DANG keeps the foundation model at inference and studies how to use its features when labels are scarce; distillation is complementary and could compress it.

\paragraph{Prototype-based segmentation.} Few-shot semantic segmentation classifies query pixels by their similarity to class prototypes computed from support images~\cite{wang2019panet}. Self-support prototypes~\cite{fan2022ssp} are built from confident features of the query image itself, which narrows the gap between support and query appearance. DANG adopts this self-support idea inside a fully supervised segmenter: learned prototypes and ground-truth anchors give a prior, and image-specific self-support centroids adapt it at test time.

\paragraph{Adaptation and directional operators.} Low-rank adaptation (LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora}), its convolutional variant for SAM (Conv-LoRA~\cite{zhong2024convlora}) and dense-prediction heads (DPT~\cite{ranftl2021vision}) adapt frozen ViTs to segmentation; DANG uses convolutional low-rank adapters and a DPT-style reassembler. State-space models (Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba}, VMamba~\cite{liu2024vmamba}, U-Mamba~\cite{ma2024umamba}) capture long-range context with directional scans; our strip operator approximates directional context with depthwise strip convolutions.

\paragraph{Robustness evaluation.} Our synthetic adverse cohort follows the corruption-benchmark design of ImageNet-C~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}: deterministic, parametrised corruptions applied only at test time.

\section{Method}
DANG maps an RGB frame $I\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times352\times352}$ to a polyp probability map $\hat Y=\sigma(Z)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). We write $X^{\uparrow n}$ for bilinear upsampling of a map $X$ to $n\times n$.

\subsection{Two Streams}
\label{sec:stem}
The local stream is ConvNeXt-Tiny, producing features $F^{(s)}$ at strides $s\in\{4,8,16,32\}$. The semantic stream is DINOv2-Base, frozen, applied to the frame resized to $350\times350$ so that its $14$-pixel patches form a $25\times25$ grid. Rank-$4$ convolutional low-rank adapters~\cite{zhong2024convlora} on its last four blocks are trained. The evaluated model also contains a Retinex chromatic stem~\cite{land1971lightness}, which removes specular highlights and divides out a smooth illumination estimate before both streams, and a style calibration of $F^{(4)}$ and $F^{(8)}$ conditioned on the global DINOv2 vector (with MixStyle~\cite{zhou2021mixstyle}). Neither is trained on corrupted images, and Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that together they give no measurable gain.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Reassembly}
We take DINOv2 tokens from blocks $\{2,5,8,11\}$ and project each to a $192$-channel map $S_\ell$ on the $25\times25$ grid. A learned depth-mixing matrix $W=\mathrm{Softmax}(\Theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times4}$, with $\Theta$ learned logits, produces one semantic map per stride,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde S_s=\sum_{\ell} W_{s,\ell}S_\ell,\qquad \hat S_s=\phi_s\bigl(\mathrm{Up}_s(\tilde S_s,F^{(s)})\bigr),
  \label{eq:dpt}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_s$ is a $3\times3$ convolution block and $\mathrm{Up}_s$ resizes to stride $s$. At strides $8$ and $16$, $\mathrm{Up}_s$ predicts $3\times3$ reassembly kernels from $F^{(s)}$, initialised to bilinear interpolation, so that upsampled semantic features align with CNN edges; at the other strides it is bilinear.

\subsection{Dual-Prototype Head}
The head concatenates the three deepest maps, projects them to $128$ channels and normalises each position to a unit embedding $e_{u,v}$. It compares $e_{u,v}$ with $K=8$ learned foreground prototypes $p_k^+$ and $K=8$ background prototypes $p_k^-$ (unit vectors, orthogonally initialised), and with a foreground anchor $a^+$ and background anchor $a^-$:
\begin{align}
  \pi_{\text{prior}}(u,v) &= \tfrac{\tau}{\beta}\Bigl(\log\textstyle\sum_k e^{\beta\langle e_{u,v},p_k^+\rangle}-\log\textstyle\sum_k e^{\beta\langle e_{u,v},p_k^-\rangle}\Bigr)\nonumber\\
  &\quad + w_{\text{a}}\bigl(\langle e_{u,v},a^+\rangle-\langle e_{u,v},a^-\rangle\bigr)+b.
  \label{eq:proto_prior}
\end{align}
The log-sum-exp with sharpness $\beta$ (fixed) is a soft maximum over prototypes; the temperature $\tau$, the bias $b$ and the bounded anchor weight $w_{\text{a}}$ are learned. During training only, $a^\pm$ are exponential moving averages of the embeddings at positions where the ground-truth mask, average-pooled to $25\times25$, is mostly foreground or mostly background. At training and test time the head then forms image-specific self-support centroids~\cite{fan2022ssp} weighted by the prior's own confidence, $c^+=\mathrm{Norm}(\sum_{u,v}q_{u,v}^2e_{u,v})$ and $c^-=\mathrm{Norm}(\sum_{u,v}(1-q_{u,v})^2e_{u,v})$ with $q=\sigma(\pi_{\text{prior}})$, and outputs the margin map
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{\text{proto}}=\pi_{\text{prior}}+w_{\text{ss}}\bigl(\langle e,c^+\rangle-\langle e,c^-\rangle\bigr),
  \label{eq:proto_total}
\end{equation}
with a learned bounded weight $w_{\text{ss}}$. Constant values are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:impl}.

\subsection{Strip Fusion and Uncertainty-Guided Decoder}
At strides $8$--$32$ the local features $F^{(s)}$ and semantic maps $\hat S_s$ are fused by a gated directional operator applied to a feature map $X$ with conditioning map $C$ from the other stream:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Strip}(X,C) &= X+g_h\odot\mathcal{K}_{7\times1}\mathcal{K}_{1\times11}(X)\nonumber\\
  &\quad +g_v\odot\mathcal{K}_{1\times7}\mathcal{K}_{11\times1}(X)\nonumber\\
  &\quad +g_d\odot\mathcal{K}_{3\times3,d=3}(X).
  \label{eq:stripssm}
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal{K}$ are depthwise convolutions (horizontal and vertical strips, and a dilated $3\times3$ branch for diagonal context) and $(g_h,g_v,g_d)=\sigma(\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times1}([X,C]))$ are gates. The top-down decoder has four stages that output logits $M_4,\dots,M_1$ at strides $32,\dots,4$. Each stage converts the upsampled logit of the coarser stage into a probability $p$, computes the boundary uncertainty $u=4p(1-p)$, which peaks on ambiguous borders where $p=0.5$, and an outer ring $\mathrm{MaxPool}_{5\times5}(p)-p$ just outside the predicted lesion, and uses both maps to gate its strip operator and a Sobel-based edge branch. The stride-$4$ decoder output is $Z_{\text{dec}}=0.8\,Z_{\text{head}}+0.2\,\pi_{\text{proto}}^{\uparrow88}$, where $Z_{\text{head}}$ combines $M_1$ with a head over all four stages.

\subsection{Prototype-Anchored Spatial Support Envelope (PASSE)}
CNN texture far from the lesion can cross the decision threshold and produce isolated false positives. PASSE builds a coarse semantic probability $P_{\text{sem}}=\sigma\bigl(\tfrac12 M_4^{\uparrow25}+\tfrac12\pi_{\text{proto}}\bigr)$, dilates it into a smooth envelope
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}=\mathrm{AvgPool}_{3\times3}\bigl(\mathrm{MaxPool}_{5\times5}(P_{\text{sem}})\bigr),
  \label{eq:passe_env}
\end{equation}
and uses both maps to modulate the decoder output:
\begin{align}
  Z = \Bigl(Z_{\text{dec}} &-\lambda_{\text{out}}\,\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(t_{\text{out}}-\mathcal{E}^{\uparrow88}\bigr)\nonumber\\
  &+\lambda_{\text{in}}\,\mathrm{ReLU}\bigl(P_{\text{sem}}^{\uparrow88}-t_{\text{in}}\bigr)\Bigr)^{\uparrow352}.
  \label{eq:passe_mod}
\end{align}
The first term lowers logits outside the envelope, and the second raises them in confident lesion cores.

\subsection{Training Objective}
DANG and our in-house controls are trained with
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L} &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE+Dice}}^{w}(Z,Y)+0.25\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{Tv}}^{w}(Z,Y)+0.1\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{far}}(Z,Y)\nonumber\\
  &\quad +\tfrac{0.25}{3}\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{4}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE+Dice}}^{w}(M_j^{\uparrow352},Y)+0.35\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{proto}}.
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{align}
Each image's terms are weighted by $w$, which grows as lesion area shrinks. The Tversky term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Tv}}$ penalises false negatives more for small lesions. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{far}}$ is the mean predicted probability outside dilations of the ground truth. The three coarser decoder logits $M_2,\dots,M_4$ receive deep supervision. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{proto}}$ applies the cross-entropy, Dice and Tversky terms to $\pi_{\text{proto}}$ against the mask average-pooled to $25\times25$ and is present only in models with the prototype head. Exact forms are in Appendix~\ref{app:impl}. The retrained published models use their official losses; one Polyp-PVT is additionally trained with Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} as a loss-matched control.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Data}
We remove from every evaluation set the images that duplicate training images, using MD5 hashes and a $64$-bit perceptual difference hash ($\text{dHash}\le4$); this includes Kvasir frames that public composites redistribute under other names. Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the cohorts. ``Tiny/camouflaged'' and ``adverse'' are diagnostic slices of the external cohorts, not independent patient cohorts, and the adverse cohort is synthetic. We write ``$40\to X$'' for a model trained on the $40$-label subset and evaluated on cohort $X$, and ``$800\to X$'' for the full training set. The $40$-label subset is a fixed $5\%$ random draw of the $800$ training images, identical for all seeds and models.

\subsection{Training and Models}
All models are trained at $352\times352$ with batch size $16$, AdamW, warm-up followed by cosine decay, gradient clipping, mixed precision, random scaling, flips, $90^\circ$ rotations and mild colour jitter, for $40$ epochs on the $40$-label subset and $20$ epochs on the $800$ images. After each epoch we evaluate both the raw weights and their exponential moving average on the validation set and keep the better checkpoint; test sets are never used for selection. Appendix~\ref{app:impl} lists all hyperparameters.

PraNet, Polyp-PVT and EMCAD use their official architectures, ImageNet-pretrained backbones (Res2Net-50 and PVTv2-B2) and official losses, with our optimiser, schedule, augmentation and evaluation. Our in-house models are a ConvNeXt-Tiny + FPN baseline; a \emph{concatenation control} that adds the same frozen DINOv2 and adapters as DANG and fuses the two streams by concatenation into an FPN decoder; DANG without the stem and style calibration; and DANG.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}
Predictions are thresholded at $0.5$. mDice is the mean of per-image Dice scores. Boundary F1 (BF1)~\cite{csurka2013good} matches predicted and ground-truth contours within $2$ pixels. Appendix~\ref{app:hd95} reports the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95).

Five seeds ($n=5$) are the unit of analysis. For a given seed, all models see the same subset and data order, so differences are tested with two-sided paired $t$-tests. For every pair of models we correct the $12$ primary comparisons (mDice, BF1 and HD95 on the three $40$-label external cohorts; mDice on $800\to$ETIS, PraNet-Ext and PolypGen-Ext) with Holm's procedure~\cite{holm1979simple}. Figure intervals are $95\%$ $t$-intervals. Inference speed is measured at batch size $1$ in FP16 on an NVIDIA T4.

\subsection{Comparison With Retrained Published Models}
\label{sec:sota}
Table~\ref{tab:sota} compares DANG with PraNet, Polyp-PVT and EMCAD retrained under our protocol.

\paragraph{$40$ labels.} DANG has the highest mDice and BF1 of all retrained models on all three external cohorts. Its margin over the best retrained model in each column is $7.1$--$7.6$~pp mDice and $4.8$--$10.0$~pp BF1; the best competitor is PraNet in four columns and a Polyp-PVT variant in two. Against the models trained with their official losses, every BF1 difference and every adverse-cohort mDice difference survives Holm correction. The tiny/camouflaged and PraNet-Ext mDice differences to Polyp-PVT do not (adjusted $p=0.085$ for each). The Polyp-PVT trained with our loss does not close the gap in mean, but its seed-to-seed SD reaches $12.7$~pp and none of its differences to DANG is significant.

\paragraph{$800$ labels.} DANG still beats PraNet on every cohort. Its differences to Polyp-PVT ($+1.4$~pp on ETIS, $+0.7$~pp on PraNet-Ext, $-0.1$~pp on PolypGen-Ext) and to EMCAD are not significant. DANG's advantage is therefore specific to the label-scarce regime.

\paragraph{Qualitative results.} Figure~\ref{fig:qual} shows predicted masks on randomly drawn frames. Every model misses the tiny ColonDB lesion. On the camouflaged, low-light and combined-corruption frames DANG reaches Dice $82\%$, $53\%$ and $60\%$, and on the low-light frame it is the only model that finds the lesion. On the NBI frame DANG ($63\%$) is below the baseline ($67\%$) and PraNet ($65\%$).

\subsection{Standard Training Split}
\label{sec:std}
Our protocol trains on Kvasir only, so its numbers are not comparable with the literature, which trains on $900$ Kvasir and $550$ ClinicDB images. To link the two, we trained DANG and Polyp-PVT on this standard split for $50$ epochs, used the final moving-average weights without any checkpoint selection, and evaluated at native resolution with three seeds (Table~\ref{tab:std}). Our Polyp-PVT reproduction is within $1.1$~pp of its published scores on every test set, which validates the pipeline. These two tests are paired over three seeds and uncorrected, and ETIS was among the cohorts inspected during design (Section~\ref{sec:limits}); the result is consistent with, but weaker evidence than, the five-seed results.

\subsection{Where the Gain Comes From}
\label{sec:ablation}
The in-house rows of Table~\ref{tab:sota} isolate the components; Appendix~\ref{app:ablation} gives the paired differences (Table~\ref{tab:diff}) and per-seed plots (Figures~\ref{fig:multiseed} and~\ref{fig:deltas}).

\paragraph{Frozen features alone do not explain the gain.} The concatenation control has the same frozen DINOv2 and adapters as DANG and $120$M parameters, yet with $40$ labels it stays at the level of the $31$M-parameter baseline ($44.9\%$ vs.\ $44.7\%$ on tiny/camouflaged, $46.3\%$ vs.\ $45.6\%$ on adverse). DANG exceeds it by $6.1$~pp ($p=0.003$) and $9.5$~pp ($p<0.001$) mDice on these cohorts and by $14.1$~pp BF1 on tiny/camouflaged lesions, all significant after correction. The gain therefore lies in the components that consume the features, the prototype head, PASSE and the strip decoder together with the prototype loss. Because the control also uses a simpler decoder, this comparison does not isolate any single component.

\paragraph{Gains over the baseline.} With $40$ labels, DANG raises BF1 by $11.9$--$14.4$~pp on all three external cohorts and mDice on the adverse cohort by $10.2$~pp, all significant after correction; the $6.3$~pp tiny/camouflaged mDice gain is not ($p=0.014$, adjusted $0.055$). With $800$ labels DANG gains $6.3$~pp on ETIS and $3.2$~pp on PraNet-Ext, and on the adverse cohort mDice rises from $55.8\%$ for the baseline to $67.4\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:multiseed}). In-domain accuracy is unchanged ($92.95\%$ vs.\ $92.92\%$ on Kvasir test). On the held-out PolypGen-Ext the gain is smaller but significant ($+1.4$~pp, $p=0.009$; $+1.2$~pp micro-averaged on PolypGen-Ext-SD, $p=0.004$).

\paragraph{The stem shows no measurable gain.} Removing the Retinex stem and style calibration keeps almost all of the improvement, and no difference between DANG and its stem-free variant survives correction. Uncorrected, the stem costs $4.3$~pp mDice on NBI-shifted frames ($p=0.008$) and adds $3.3$~pp BF1 on tiny/camouflaged lesions ($p=0.022$). A plausible reason for the NBI loss, which we did not test, is that the stem standardises colour towards white-light Kvasir statistics. We report the full model as DANG because all main experiments use it, but recommend the stem-free variant, which is also faster.

\subsection{Efficiency}
DANG is the most expensive model in Table~\ref{tab:sota}: $129.9$M parameters, of which $86.6$M are frozen DINOv2 weights, and $17.4$~FPS against $36.5$--$42.4$~FPS for the retrained models. The concatenation control runs the same two encoders at $38.4$~FPS, so most of DANG's extra latency lies in its decoding path rather than in the foundation model. At $17$--$19$~FPS DANG suits second-reader and offline review. Real-time use at $25$--$30$~Hz would need a faster GPU, an optimised decoding path, or distillation into a smaller network~\cite{agnihotri2025difom,agnihotri2026litepi}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Training length of the retrained models.} With $40$ labels the retrained models reached their best validation score in the last $5$ of $40$ epochs and may not have converged; DANG peaked between epochs $25$ and $39$. Longer training could narrow the gap.
  \item \textbf{One subset, one source centre.} The $40$-image subset is the same for all seeds, so the reported variance reflects optimisation, not the choice of images; all main models are trained on Kvasir only.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive design.} PASSE thresholds and loss constants were tuned while inspecting PolypGen-Dev, ETIS and the two stress cohorts, so these are not fully held out; this includes ETIS, the cohort of the standard-split result. PolypGen-Ext was not used for design, and gains there are smaller.
  \item \textbf{Shared sequences.} $74$ PolypGen-Ext frames share video sequences with PolypGen-Dev; PolypGen-Ext-SD removes them.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic and derived cohorts.} The adverse cohort is synthetic rather than paired physical NBI/white-light acquisitions, and both stress cohorts are slices of the external cohorts.
  \item \textbf{Statistical power.} Five seeds give four degrees of freedom and the standard-split experiment three; several raw $p<0.05$ effects do not survive correction.
  \item \textbf{Confounded prototype loss.} Models with the prototype head also receive $\mathcal{L}_{\text{proto}}$, and we have not run a factorial over PASSE, the prototype loss and the strip decoder.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} We evaluate single frames, not video, and report no clinical reader study.
  \item \textbf{Cost.} DANG is $2$--$2.5\times$ slower than the retrained models and has $4$--$5\times$ more parameters.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
DANG uses a frozen DINOv2 through a prototype margin head, a prototype-anchored support envelope and an uncertainty-guided strip decoder. Trained on $40$ labelled images it outperforms PraNet, Polyp-PVT and EMCAD retrained under the same protocol on every external cohort, by at least $7.1$~pp mDice and $4.8$~pp BF1 over the best of them, and on the standard split it matches Polyp-PVT on four test sets and exceeds it on ETIS. A same-size concatenation control shows that the frozen features alone do not explain the gain. With $800$ labels DANG ties the best retrained models, and it is $2$--$2.5\times$ slower. Next steps are longer training of the baselines in the label-scarce regime, resampled subsets, a lighter backbone and decoding path, and evaluation on physical NBI acquisitions and video.

\section{HD95 and Empty Predictions}
\label{app:hd95}
When a prediction is empty and the ground truth is not, HD95 is undefined; we assign a quarter of the image diagonal, $0.25\cdot352\sqrt2\approx124.5$~px. This penalty is smaller than the HD95 of $10.9\%$ of non-empty predictions, so an empty mask can score better than a mask with a distant false positive. Empty-prediction rates are similar for DANG and the baseline ($28.5\%$ vs.\ $27.8\%$ on $40\to$tiny/camouflaged). With half- or full-diagonal penalties the direction of the HD95 differences holds but their size shrinks, and on two cohorts they are not significant even with the default penalty (Table~\ref{tab:diff}). We therefore base our claims on mDice and BF1.
